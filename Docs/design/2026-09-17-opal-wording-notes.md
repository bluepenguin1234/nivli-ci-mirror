# Opal wording notes — what to borrow, what to leave

2026-09-17. Read of `opalapp.com`, `/features` and `/pricing` for the marketing-voice pass.
Companion to `2026-09-17-opal-direction.md`, which covers the visual side. This one is copy only.

Everything quoted below is Opal's own marketing, recorded here as evidence. None of it is ours
to ship.

## 10 observations

1. **The problem is stated as a number before it is stated as a feeling.** The first thing on
   the page is a count of hours, not a word like "distraction" or "doomscrolling". The hours do
   the arguing; the adjective would only be a claim. Nivli should do the same: lead with the
   number, then say what it costs you, then say what to do about it.

2. **The number is framed as *your* day, not as a study.** Opal writes the stat in the second
   person and in the present tense — the time you'll spend *today* — so it reads as a forecast
   rather than a fact about strangers. That is the whole trick, and it is the one thing worth
   copying structurally. We copy the *structure* (second person, today, forecast), not the
   sentence.

3. **The promise is a state, not a feature.** "Attention on autopilot." The headline names what
   you get to stop doing, not what the app does. Nivli's equivalent already exists and is
   better: "Move first. Then scroll." — a rule, in four words, that also tells you the mechanic.
   Do not replace it.

4. **Sentences are short, second person, and declarative.** No hedging, no "helps you to", no
   "designed to". Verbs are plain and physical: *block*, *set*, *lock in*, *unlock*, *tap into*.
   Nivli's voice should stay in the same register: *lock*, *move*, *log it*, *open*, *wait*.

5. **Feature names are nouns, and the line under them is a verb phrase.** "Focus Rules® — Set
   your Rules." Heading names the thing, subhead tells you the action. Our three website cards
   already follow this; the fix is to shorten the body under each one, not to restructure them.

6. **Benefit copy is cause-and-effect in one breath.** "Choose what you want to focus on, set the
   length, and lock in until the timer runs out." One sentence, three beats, ends in the payoff.
   The paywall rows should read the same way: what happens, then when it ends.

7. **CTA verbs are low-commitment and first person to the reader.** "Try for free", "Try Opal for
   free". They never say "Subscribe", "Buy" or "Get started with a plan". Nivli has no free tier,
   so we cannot say "try for free" — and must not imply it. Our honest equivalents are
   *Download on the App Store*, *Get started*, *Start for $2.99/month*. Keep the price visible
   next to the verb; that is our differentiator, not a disclosure we hide.

8. **They *do* source the headline efficacy numbers — once, in small type.** "1h 23m saved
   daily", "1 month saved each year", "6 years of life reclaimed" carry a footnote: calculated
   from Screen Time before and after Opal for N=300,000. The outcome percentages ("94% Less
   distracted", "93% More productive", "90% Improved mental health") appear without a visible
   method. So: sourcing is inconsistent even at their scale, and the unsourced ones are exactly
   the ones that would be hardest to defend. Lesson: if we cite it, we say where it came from in
   the same eyeful, or we do not cite it.

9. **Restraint is the tone.** Whole sections carry one line of copy. Nothing is explained twice,
   and there is no paragraph anywhere on the home page longer than two sentences. Nivli's pages
   are wordier than this, and the two places it is *correct* to be wordier are the "what it
   cannot do" paragraph and the privacy paragraph — those are promises, and promises need their
   qualifiers. Everything else should get shorter.

10. **The hard edge is sold as a feature, not apologised for.** "Hard Mode — No way out. You
    can't turn Opal off." Nivli's honest position is the opposite ("you can delete it at any
    moment") and we must keep saying so, but we can borrow the confidence: state the constraint
    flatly, in the affirmative, as the point of the product. "Your apps wait until you've moved"
    is a constraint sold as a benefit. That is the register.

## What NOT to copy

- **Their stat sentence, verbatim.** Opal's home page reads: "5 to 6 hours. That's the average
  time you'll spend on your phone today — often without realizing." That is their copy. Our
  number is the same publicly-quoted range, but the sentence must be ours. See the flag below.
- **"Attention on autopilot", "Focus Rules", "Focus Timer", "Focus Gems", "Opal Score", "Hard
  Mode".** Several carry ® on their own site. None of these words go near our listing, our
  keywords or our app.
- **Any efficacy claim.** "1h 23m saved daily", "1 month saved each year", "6 years of life
  reclaimed", "94% less distracted", "93% more productive", "90% improved mental health". We
  have no users, no cohort, no before/after data and no basis for any of it. Nivli must not
  claim a health, productivity or mental-health outcome in any form, including softened ones
  ("feel sharper", "get your time back"). The App Store rejects unsubstantiated health claims,
  and we would deserve it.
- **Rating and social proof.** "4.8 · 150k+ App Ratings", "This app has changed my life". We
  have neither the ratings nor the testimonials. Nothing invented, nothing implied.
- **"Try for free" and any free-tier framing.** There is no free tier. Saying or implying one
  is a guideline 2.3.1 problem and a refund problem.
- **Registered-mark styling on our own feature words.** We have no registrations. No ® anywhere.
- **The urgency/loss register** ("reclaim your life", "years lost"). Nivli's whole posture is
  that it is a speed bump you agreed to, not a rescue. Keep it calm.

## The one flag for the owner

The brief's phrasing of the stat is very close to Opal's own sentence. Ours reads better and
carries the honesty we want, but the *opening* is near-identical in shape:

- Opal: "5 to 6 hours. That's the average time you'll spend on your phone today…"
- Ours (web eyebrow, as briefed): "5 to 6 hours. That's your phone today."

Shipped as-is, per the brief. If the owner would rather not stand that close, the drop-in
replacements that keep the number and lose the echo are:

- Web eyebrow: **"The average phone day: 5 to 6 hours."**
- App Store promotional text opener: **"The average phone day runs 5 to 6 hours."**

The in-app card is already clear of it ("5–6 h / is what the average person spends on their
phone today."), because it moves the number out of the sentence and into the numeral.

Separately: the stat itself is an industry-estimate range, not a figure we measured. The in-app
card states it as an average, which is the honest framing. If the owner wants explicit
attribution, the one-line drop-in is a caption reading **"Industry estimates. Your own number is
in Screen Time."** — no invented source, no named study.
